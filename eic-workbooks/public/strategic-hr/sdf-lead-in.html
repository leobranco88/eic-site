01 · The Decision

Have you ever tried to change someone's mind?

Not in a formal presentation. In a real conversation, with someone who had the power to say yes or no, and where it actually mattered.

01
What makes it easy to get approval from someone senior, and what makes it hard?
Is it always about the argument, or is something else usually at play?
Some situations to consider
  • A manager needed headcount approved during a budget freeze — the argument was solid but the timing was wrong
  • Someone proposed a new process right after a visible failure — the same proposal that had been rejected twice before
  • A leader got a yes in a hallway conversation that had been blocked in three formal meetings
02
What makes someone's decision-making hard to read?
Is it the person, the moment, or the framing that makes the difference?
Some situations to consider
  • Someone who always asks "who owns this?" before engaging with any idea
  • A leader whose decisions seem to depend on who brought the proposal, not what it contains
  • A director who approves things quickly in one-on-ones but stalls everything in group meetings
03
What should you think about before walking into a conversation with your CEO, before deciding what to say?
What is it about how they decide that does not follow a clear pattern?
Some situations to consider
  • Thinking about whether they are likely to be distracted, pressured, or in a good headspace
  • Considering what they care about most — and what opening sentence would connect to that
  • Deciding whether to bring data, a story, or a direct ask — based on how they typically engage
04
Why does the same idea land well with one person and fail completely with another?
Same idea. Same data. Different outcome. What was different about the two people?
Some situations to consider
  • A CFO approved something quickly when it was framed as cost reduction — and had rejected the same idea when it was framed as a people initiative
  • A CEO ignored detailed data but responded immediately to a one-line connection to the company's growth plan
  • Two directors in the same meeting reacted completely differently to the same proposal — one focused on the numbers, the other on who was in the room
02 · The Moment

Timing is not luck. It is a skill.

The same proposal gets approved or rejected depending on when it lands. Most people focus on what they say. Not when they say it.

05
What role does timing play in whether a proposal gets approved? Does the content matter more or less than the moment?
Not the content of the proposal. The context around it.
Some situations to consider
  • A proposal that had been stalled for months moved in one week after a key person resigned
  • An idea rejected in Q1 was approved in Q3 with almost no changes — the organisation had shifted
  • A conversation that failed in a formal meeting succeeded two days later over coffee
06
What signals tell you that people inside an organisation are more open to change at a given moment?
What context around a proposal tends to matter most?
Some situations to consider
  • Right after a restructuring — people are rethinking processes and open to new structures
  • When a competitor makes a visible move — suddenly the status quo feels risky
  • In the first 90 days of a new leader — before positions are fixed and before politics are fully formed
07
What stops people from bringing important things to leadership early enough?
What creates those openings, and what closes them?
Some situations to consider
  • Waiting until the analysis is perfect — and by then the decision has already been made
  • Not feeling senior enough to raise something that belongs at a higher level
  • Believing the timing is wrong — and never finding a moment that feels right enough

Who you talk to first changes everything.

The decision is rarely made in the room where it is presented. It is made in the conversations that happen before that room.

04 · Your Experience

What does influence look like in your company?

These questions do not have right answers. They are about your reality, your company, your people.

08
What makes a decision move quickly and smoothly. Why do you think it moved so fast?
What was different about that situation compared to others where things stalled?
Some situations to consider
  • The right person championed it informally before it ever reached a formal meeting
  • It solved a problem that leadership was already feeling — so there was no resistance to overcome
  • It was framed as a small next step, not a big change — which made it easy to say yes to
09
What types of people are hardest to convince, and what makes them that way?
What is usually behind that delay?
Some situations to consider
  • People whose first instinct is to protect what already exists rather than evaluate what is being proposed
  • People who need to trust the person before they can trust the idea
  • People who have been burned by similar proposals before and carry that experience into every new conversation
10
When does it make sense to change your approach in the middle of a conversation, and when is it too late?
What conditions make a decision move easily?
Some situations to consider
  • When you notice the person is not responding to logic — and you shift to a story or a concrete example instead
  • When an unexpected objection surfaces and you realise your framing needs to change
  • When the energy in the room drops and you decide to ask a question instead of continuing to present
11
What matters more in a high-stakes conversation: the quality of your argument or the relationship you have with the person?
What is it that makes some people consistently resistant?
Some situations to consider
  • A technically weak proposal that went through because the person trusted who brought it
  • A strong, well-researched proposal that stalled because the relationship was not established first
  • A situation where the argument was perfect but the person in the room was not ready to hear it from that specific person
