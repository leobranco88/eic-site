The Duarte Sparkline — how great narratives move
Hook
Problem
Insight
Solution
Evidence
Vision
Action
01
Hook
Comes first because the brain only opens to new information when it feels the current state is inadequate. No discomfort, no opening.
why?
The audience needs to recognize themselves in the problem before they can feel the gap. Hook without Status Quo feels abstract.
02
Status Quo
Comes second because before accepting something is wrong, the audience must see themselves in the description. Otherwise, it feels like someone else's problem.
why?
You can only subvert a belief that has been established. The Gap requires the Status Quo to exist first — otherwise there is nothing to contradict.
03
Gap
Comes third because contradiction only lands when the audience knows the territory. The Gap subverts a belief — you can only subvert what has already been established.
why?
The Insight is the emotional turning point. It only has weight after three slides of problem. Arriving early, it feels lightweight. Arriving here, it feels earned.
04
Insight
The turning point. The audience has suffered through three slides of problem. The Insight arrives as genuine relief — the first moment of possibility. This is the Sparkline pivot.
why?
A solution without an insight is a pitch. With the insight established, the solution feels like a logical destination the audience was already heading toward.
05
Solution
Comes fifth because the audience is now emotionally ready. They have felt the problem and understood the reframe. The solution arrives as a conclusion, not a proposal.
why?
The audience accepts the solution emotionally in slide 5 — but the rational brain immediately questions it. Evidence answers that objection before it is verbalized.
06
Evidence
Comes sixth because rational persuasion must follow emotional acceptance — never precede it. Data presented before the audience cares is ignored. Data presented after they believe is confirmation.
why?
After rational proof, the audience is ready for the final emotional move. You first convince, then inspire. Vision before evidence feels premature. Vision after evidence feels inevitable.
07
Vision
Comes seventh because inspiration only works after belief. The audience has accepted the solution and seen the proof. Now they can imagine — and imagination is what creates motivation to act.
why?
A request only works when the audience has already reached the same conclusion as you. Asked before, it is pressure. Asked after, it is facilitating a decision they already made.
08
Action
Comes last because the ask only works after the audience has arrived at the same conclusion. Asking early is pressure. Asking here is simply opening the door.

The eight moments
of a great arc.

Every strong presentation moves through these moments in sequence. Understanding what each slide must do is the foundation for everything that follows.

01
Hook
What Is
Create a gap between what the audience currently believes and what they are about to discover.
The audience should feel: unsettled, curious, slightly uncomfortable.
Do not introduce the solution. Do not soften the data. The hook works because it is uncomfortable.
02
Status Quo
What Is
Make the current state feel recognizable, inadequate, and structurally flawed — not a personal failure.
The audience should feel: recognized, validated, and mildly frustrated.
Frame the problem as a design flaw, never as someone's fault. The audience must see themselves in the description.
03
Gap / Conflict
What Is
Expose the contradiction between what the audience believed was the solution and what the evidence actually shows.
The audience should feel: their mental model shifting. An "aha" that creates readiness for the insight.
Name the wrong assumption first, then introduce the contradiction. Never rush to the solution here.
04
Insight
Turning Point
Deliver the reframe that makes a different solution not just possible but inevitable. This is the Sparkline turning point.
The audience should feel: the first genuine sense of possibility. A combination of "of course" and "we never thought of it this way."
Use a metaphor or a sharp contrast. Never lead with data here. The insight must feel discovered, not presented.
05
Solution
What Could Be
Show the design or proposal as a logical consequence of the insight — not a pitch, but a destination the audience has already been heading toward.
The audience should feel: relief. The answer has arrived exactly when it should.
Lead with outcomes, not features. Get to evidence fast — every second spent elaborating is a second in which skepticism grows.
06
Evidence
What Could Be
Prove the solution works with specific, credible data — then immediately make that data personally relevant to this audience.
The audience should feel: trust. The claim is now a fact.
Specificity is credibility. Named sectors, exact numbers, time-bounded results. Avoid "many clients" or "significant improvement."
07
Vision
What Could Be
Invite the audience to construct an emotionally compelling picture of the future — then immediately ground it in the next step.
The audience should feel: hopeful and ready to act. Not excited to the point of skepticism.
Use imagine, not picture this. Stay short — one paragraph. The vision is a peak, not a plateau. Bridge to action immediately.
08
Action / Ask
New Bliss
Make the narrowest possible ask that moves things forward — then close with a calibrated question, not a summary.
The audience should feel: that saying no is harder than saying yes.
Never summarize. Never say any questions? Close with: What would you need to see to feel confident about the next step?

Six types of bridge.
Learn them first.

Every transition in a presentation does one of these six jobs. Know the job before you practice the phrase. Click any type to see examples from your theme.

F
Frame
Sets up what is about to happen. The audience gets a map before the journey starts.
C
Cause and Effect
Connects a problem to its consequence. The audience follows the logic.
C
Contrast
Pivots from one idea to its opposite. Creates the tension that makes a reframe land.
Z
Zoom
Moves from big picture to detail, or signals that one specific thing is about to matter.
P
Payoff
Signals arrival. Everything before was building to this. The audience leans in.
Q
Question Shift
Changes the question the audience is holding. The mental model has updated.
Step 2 · Warm Up · 4 of 4

Where this
comes from.

Every framework in this simulator is grounded in research on communication, cognition, and persuasion. These are the primary sources.

Nancy Duarte
2010
Resonate: Present Visual Stories that Transform Audiences
The Sparkline structure — the oscillation between "what is" and "what could be" — is the foundation of the arc used in every theme of this simulator. Duarte's analysis of hundreds of speeches, including Steve Jobs' iPhone keynote and Martin Luther King's "I Have a Dream," established the pattern of tension and release that drives great presentations.
Used for: Slide type sequence, arc structure, Hook and Vision slide principles.
Barbara Minto
1987
The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking
Minto's work at McKinsey established the principle that defines executive communication: lead with the conclusion, then justify it. The PREP structure, the "Bottom line upfront" approach, and the logic behind Frame and Payoff bridges all derive from the Pyramid Principle. It also informs the Action slide's narrow-ask design.
Used for: Frame bridge type, Action slide structure, PREP anchor expressions, closing question design.
Chip Heath and Dan Heath
2007
Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die
The Heath brothers' research on "sticky" communication established the role of contrast, unexpectedness, and concrete specificity in making ideas memorable. Their concept of the "knowledge gap" directly informs the Hook and Gap slide types. The SUCCESs framework (Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Story) underlies the evidence and vision slide principles.
Used for: Contrast bridge type, Hook and Gap slide design, Evidence slide specificity principle.
Aristotle
c. 350 BCE
Rhetoric
The three modes of persuasion — Ethos (credibility), Pathos (emotion), and Logos (logic) — map directly onto the arc structure. The diagnostic slides (Status Quo, Gap, Insight) build Logos. The Vision slide builds Pathos. The proactive risk disclosure and evidence specificity build Ethos. Aristotle's taxonomy of logical connectors also informs the Cause and Effect bridge type.
Used for: Cause and Effect bridge type, arc balance between logic and emotion, credibility-building techniques.
Robert Cialdini
1984
Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion
Cialdini's principles of social proof, reciprocity, and commitment inform specific techniques across the arc. The proactive risk disclosure (surfacing risks before the audience asks) applies reciprocity. The narrow ask at the closing slide uses commitment and consistency — making the first yes small so subsequent yeses follow naturally.
Used for: Risk slide structure, Action slide narrow-ask design, proactive objection handling.
HBR and McKinsey Quarterly
2010 — 2023
Executive Communication Research Corpus
The C1 corporate expressions in this simulator were validated against a corpus of executive presentations, board-level pitch decks, and advisory communication published by HBR, McKinsey, and BCG. Phrases like "which brings me to," "the headline is," "bottom line," and "that gets at something important" recur consistently in high-stakes business communication across US and UK contexts.
Used for: All bridge expressions, power phrases, C1 register calibration.
Ebbinghaus, H.
1885
Über das Gedächtnis (Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology)
The forgetting curve — the finding that memory decays exponentially without reinforcement — is the scientific basis for the spaced repetition and retrieval practice design of the flashcard system. The "memory is a muscle" metaphor used in the Learning Solution theme is a pedagogically valid representation of Ebbinghaus's findings on active recall vs passive review.
Used for: Flashcard design rationale, spaced repetition logic, Learning Solution insight slide.
EIC · Escola de Idiomas e Cultura
2026
Solution Adviser Track · Classroom Observation
The slide dependency problem — the pattern of presenters stopping, resetting, and starting fresh at each slide — was identified through direct classroom observation at EIC. The specific error patterns used in the three-choice bridge exercise (correct, plausible, weak) were designed from recurring student mistakes observed in C1 presentation practice sessions.
Used for: Simulator design rationale, choice exercise calibration, instructor debrief questions.
Step 2 · Warm Up · 5 of 4

Terms worth
knowing.

The expressions in this simulator use technical vocabulary from business communication, learning science, and corporate strategy. These are the terms that carry the most weight.

Transfer Learning Science
From Latin transferre — to carry across.
The ability to apply what was learned in a training context to a real work situation. The gap between knowing something and using it. Most corporate training fails at transfer — the knowledge stays in the room.
In business presenting, naming "transfer" signals that you understand the root of the problem, not just the symptom. It shifts the conversation from content quality to system design.
"The architecture was never designed for transfer."
Architecture Strategy
From Greek arkhitekton — master builder.
In business, architecture means the underlying structure of a system — how its parts connect and what they are optimized for. Unlike a "program" (a single intervention), an architecture implies intentional, systemic design.
Saying "architecture" instead of "system" or "approach" implies rigor and permanence. It also implies a designer — which means the problem is fixable by redesigning.
"A retention architecture is a system built into the operating rhythm."
Bottom Line Executive Communication
From accounting — the final line of a profit and loss statement showing net result.
In business communication, "bottom line" means the essential point, the conclusion, the thing that matters most after all the detail is stripped away. It signals: I am cutting to what you actually need to know.
It is ubiquitous in US corporate settings because it respects the audience's time and signals confidence. Always follow it immediately with the conclusion, never with more context.
"Bottom line: this works. Here is the proof."
Flag Corporate Idiom
From the literal act of placing a flag to mark something important on a map.
In corporate communication, "to flag" means to mark something as important, to draw attention to it deliberately. It signals to the audience: pay particular attention to what comes next, or: I am noting this so we can return to it.
It is a verb of control — the presenter decides what gets flagged. It creates emphasis without repetition and is far more sophisticated than "I want to highlight" or "I want to point out."
"I want to flag this number before we move on."
Park British Corporate Idiom
From the act of leaving a car in a designated place to return to later.
In business meetings and presentations, "to park" something means to set it aside temporarily with the intention of returning to it. It acknowledges a question or topic without derailing the current flow.
Very common in UK corporate environments. It signals structure and control — the presenter is managing the agenda, not avoiding the question. More elegant than "let's come back to that."
"I want to park the big picture for a moment and look at one specific detail."
Headline Executive Communication
From journalism — the title above a news article that summarizes the story.
In McKinsey and BCG communication style, "the headline" of a slide is its single main point — the conclusion that would be true even if someone only read the title. Every slide should have one headline. If you cannot state it, the slide is not ready.
Using "the headline is" in a presentation signals that you think in structured, conclusive terms. It replaces "the main point is" with something that feels sharper and more deliberate.
"The headline here is simple: this approach costs less and produces more."
Legible Strategy Communication
From Latin legibilis — able to be read.
In business strategy, "legible" means clear, understandable, and easy to evaluate. A strategy is legible when its logic is visible — when you can see why it will work without having to trust blindly. The opposite is opaque or complex.
Using "legible" instead of "clear" or "simple" signals analytical sophistication. It implies that the work has been done to make the complexity accessible, not that the idea itself is simple.
"With those risks accounted for, the strategy becomes much more legible."
Traction Startup and Sales
From physics — the grip between a surface and a moving object.
In business, traction means measurable evidence that a product, strategy, or idea is working in the real world — not in theory. Revenue growth, user adoption, retention rates, NRR. Traction is the proof that something has moved from concept to reality.
The word implies momentum — things are moving, not static. In a pitch or proposal, "the traction is there" is a declarative fact, not a claim. It signals that the conversation has moved from whether something works to how to scale it.
"So the traction is there. Not as a claim. As a measurement."
NRR — Net Revenue Retention SaaS and Finance
The percentage of revenue retained from existing customers after accounting for expansions, downgrades, and churn.
NRR above 100% means customers are spending more over time, not less — even without counting new customers. It is the single most powerful signal of product value in subscription businesses. NRR of 138% means for every $100 a cohort paid last year, they paid $138 this year.
In a pitch, citing NRR signals financial fluency and product strength simultaneously. It is the metric that sophisticated investors and executives pay most attention to because it cannot be faked by acquisition spend.
"Net revenue retention is 138%."
Playbook Strategy and Operations
From American football — the book of plays a team uses in a game.
In business, a playbook is a documented set of strategies, processes, and tactics that an organization uses to achieve a specific goal. A "market entry playbook" is the standard approach a company uses when entering a new market. Playbooks are designed for repeatability.
The word carries connotations of preparation, experience, and competitive advantage. When a presenter says "the wrong playbook," they imply that a right one exists — which sets up the solution naturally.
"They applied a Western market entry playbook to a fundamentally different consumer ecosystem."
Runway Startup and Finance
From aviation — the strip of ground an aircraft uses to take off.
In business, runway originally meant the amount of time a startup has before running out of cash. More broadly, it now means the preparation or groundwork that makes a next step possible. "Clearing the runway" means removing obstacles before the main event.
The metaphor is intuitive and efficient. It implies forward motion and preparation without overexplaining. In presenting, "clearing the runway" (by addressing risks before the strategy) signals that the presenter is thinking two steps ahead.
"Risk to strategy should feel like clearing the runway before takeoff."
Calibrated Executive Communication
From the calibration of a measuring instrument — adjusted for precision.
In communication, a "calibrated question" is one that is carefully designed to produce useful information or move a conversation forward — as opposed to a generic question like "any questions?" A calibrated question assumes engagement and directs the answer toward a specific outcome.
The word signals intentionality. It tells the audience that the question was not improvised — it was chosen to do specific work. Chris Voss uses the term in negotiation; it has since entered broader executive communication vocabulary.
"Close with a calibrated question that already moves the conversation forward."
Flight Risk HR and Talent Management
From aviation — an aircraft or person at risk of departing unexpectedly.
In HR, a flight risk is an employee who shows indicators of being likely to leave the organization in the near term. These indicators include below-market compensation, low engagement scores, long tenure without promotion, or reduced performance. Flight risk modeling is a standard tool in people analytics.
The term is precise and professional — it avoids vague language like "might leave" and positions the concern as measurable and manageable. Using it signals that the presenter is speaking the language of data-driven HR, not intuition.
"Three of your twelve highest-impact managers show three or more flight risk indicators."
Extractive vs Generative Systems Thinking
Extractive: taking value out. Generative: producing value back.
In systems thinking, an extractive system takes resources from participants without returning equivalent value. A generative system produces more value for participants than it consumes. Applied to CRMs: an extractive CRM takes data from reps (manual entry) without returning useful information to them. A generative CRM would surface insights, actions, and proposals automatically.
The contrast is intellectually precise and immediately intuitive. It reframes the entire CRM problem in four words — from "bad tool" to "wrong value exchange" — which is exactly the kind of reframe that earns credibility in a technical pitch.
"The system is extractive, not generative."
Thread Narrative and Communication
From weaving — the continuous strand that holds a fabric together.
In communication, "the thread" is the continuous narrative line that connects all parts of a presentation. "Following the thread" means tracking the logical and emotional progression from beginning to end. "Finishing the thread" means completing a line of argument before starting a new one. Losing the thread means the audience has lost the connection between ideas.
The metaphor is physical and intuitive. It implies that narrative has structural integrity — that ideas are woven together, not stacked. Using it signals that the presenter thinks about communication architecturally, not just informationally.
"Let me finish this thread and we will go there."
